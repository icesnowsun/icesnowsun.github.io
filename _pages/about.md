I am a biostatistician in the Division of Nephrology at Stanford School of
Medicine. My work is about learning from imperfect clinical and population
health data — and, increasingly, about the methodological choices that quietly
determine what we conclude from it.

Three questions run through most of what I do. **How do we model a disease
trajectory?** In a cohort followed for chronic kidney disease of uncertain
etiology in Sri Lanka, comparing OLS, linear mixed-effects, and latent class
mixed-effects approaches to the same eGFR data sorts patients differently — the
share identified as rapid decliners shifts substantially depending on the model
alone, and on whether it is fitted pooled or stratified. **Who does a cohort
actually represent?** Similarity-based generalizability scores computed on
aggregate demographics can look reassuring while concealing which subgroups are
missing. **And when is a model ready to inform care?** That question has pulled
me toward the responsible evaluation of predictive models and AI in healthcare.

The applications so far have been in chronic kidney disease, sickle cell
disease, and national COVID-19 research cohorts — including a nationwide
cluster randomized trial across dialysis facilities and a study of dialysis
facility closures that was selected as a
[2026 AJKD Editors' Choice](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2025.12.003) article.

Alongside the analysis I build things that let non-statisticians do more for
themselves: reusable R and SAS workflows, and a browser-based flowchart tool
for clinical fellows without coding experience. I mentor summer research
students through Stanford's HBMC-REACH post-baccalaureate program and chair the
communications subcommittee of the
[Quantitative Staff Network](https://www.quantitativestaffnetwork.org/).

I trained in biostatistics and epidemiology at Georgetown and in financial
mathematics at Hong Kong Baptist University, and worked at the University of
Mississippi Medical Center before coming to Stanford.
